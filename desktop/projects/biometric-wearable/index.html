BIOMETRIC WEARABLE

PPG + IMU wearable - ESP32 POC to a 6-layer nRF52840 EVT

The Build

The project

A biometric wearable - optical heart rate (PPG), motion, and skin temperature from a board small enough to forget you are wearing. The plan was two builds: a quick POC to prove the sensing chain end to end, then an EVT that turns it into an actual wearable. Both went through fab, assembly, and bring-up inside three months.

The POC (Jan 2025)

The POC has one job: prove the sensing chain - optical heart rate, motion, temperature - off a moving body, on the shortest possible schedule. Every decision follows from that. An ESP32-S3-WROOM module (16 MB flash, 2 MB PSRAM) streams the sensors straight over Wi-Fi so firmware and algorithms develop against live data from day one; a module instead of a chip-down radio because certification and RF layout are not what a POC is for; 4 layers because that is all it needs. Boards came back in January, and the fab sent X-rays of the assembly - leadless and bottom-terminated packages hide their joints, so optical inspection alone cannot tell you much about them.

Production X-ray, Jan 13 2025 - module, QFNs, and connector joints inspected.

Remarks on circuit

Input power enters over USB-C: a 5.1 k CC resistor advertises 3 A capability, with a 2 A-hold / 4 A-trip resettable polyfuse, a reverse-polarity crowbar diode, and TVS arrays on VBUS and D± defining what actually reaches the board. A BQ25606 provides single-cell 2 A charging with power-path management, so the system runs while charging; charge current is resistor-programmed (677/R) to 1C for the 500 mAh cell. A BQ27421 fuel gauge performs coulomb counting. Three rails derive from VSYS: a TPS63021 buck-boost generates the 3V3 system rail (a Li-Po crosses 3.3 V over its discharge range), a TPS61023 boost generates 5 V for MAX30101 LED drive (green-LED peaks ~150 mA, intermittent), and a TPS7A02 low-IQ LDO generates 1V8 for the MAX30101 analog core (~2 mA load; dissipation (3.3 - 1.8) x I). The LED rail is kept separate from the analog core rail to isolate LED switching transients from the photodiode measurement path.

The ESP32-S3-WROOM-1 (N16R2) provides Wi-Fi/BLE and application compute. MAX30101 (0x57), MPU-6050 (0x68), TMP116 (0x48), and the BQ27421 (0x55) share a single I2C bus with pull-ups placed at the microcontroller; USB D± route as an approximately 50-ohm pair. Passives are 0402/0603, with 0603 preferred where rework was expected. The schematic carries its own margin notes - JST battery polarity (not standardized between vendors), ESP32 strapping-pin behavior for production programming.

Root sheet - ESP32-S3, sensor bus (MAX30101, MPU-6050, TMP116), reset.

Power sheet - USB-C input protection, BQ25606 charger with power-path, BQ27421 fuel gauge.

Rails sheet - TPS63021 3V3 buck-boost, TPS61023 5V LED boost, TPS7A02 1V8 LDO.

Firmware was ESP-IDF. By the end the pipeline was doing the real thing: PPG converting to heart rate and SpO2 on the fly, and the accelerometer stream translating into athletic measures - step counts to start.

The EVT (Mar 2025)

The POC did its job, and it made the problems obvious: streaming over Wi-Fi eats the battery, and a wearable you have to charge twice a day is really a bench instrument. So the EVT flips the architecture - log locally, sync over BLE when it is worth waking the radio. An nRF52840 (BC840 module) replaces the ESP32, sensor sessions land in a 256-Mbit QSPI flash, and the whole thing fits in 23.5 x 22.25 mm. I designed this board end to end.

EVT board render with dimensions - 23.5 x 22.25 mm.

Remarks on circuit

Power management consolidates into a Nordic nPM1300: USB input, single-cell charging, power-path, fuel gauging, and two buck regulators in one device. The 1V8 domain is split into separate analog and digital rails to keep converter switching noise out of the optical front end; an I2C level shifter bridges the low-voltage and 3V3 domains. The biosensing bus is written for up to four MAX30101 sites behind an I2C multiplexer (two populated on this build), with sensor interrupts routed to the MCU; the BMI270 IMU sits on a dedicated SPI bus and provides motion-interrupt wake from a microamp idle. The nRF52840 (BC840 module) runs two I2C masters (TWIM0/TWIM1), keeping the sensor and UI domains on separate buses; session data logs to a 256-Mbit quad-SPI flash. The UI comprises a CY8CMBR3108 CapSense controller driving brass touch pads fabricated in the board panel, a tact switch, and a 24-pin FPC to an OLED daughter board biased by an LT3494 boost. A TC2030 Tag-Connect footprint provides SWD debug; a 32.768 kHz crystal provides the RTC reference.

The stack-up is six copper layers with 0201 passives and BGA/DFN packaging throughout. The radio module mounts on exposed lands; solder-paste apertures for the unpopulated module footprints were specified as custom stencil openings with the fab. Two build revisions (3-21 and 3-26) were released within one week; the run was 50 bare boards with 5 assembled.

EVT board with its 3.7 V 30 mAh Li-Po, in palm for scale.

Encapsulated for wear.

Firmware moved to Zephyr. The CapSense pads for touch control worked, but the display wiring got messed up, and the OLED never ran right.

The enclosure is printed on a Formlabs Form 4 in BioMed Elastic 50A; at Shore 50A it is about as close as printable materials get to the 00-30 hardness of human skin, soft enough to wear the board directly.

Using atopile

There is no drawn schematic for this board. I was convinced to try representing the circuit as code instead: the design is written in atopile - five modules (Biosensing, HCI, Logic, Power, OLED daughter) that compile down to the KiCad layout. The advantages are real: the design scales like software, with modules, reuse, diffs, and review, and it is a natural fit for LLM assistance, since a language model reads and writes connectivity fluently as text. The power interface declares the rail architecture directly: 

power_interface = new PowerInterface
power_interface.vbus_out ~ vbus_out
power_interface.vsys ~ vsys
power_interface.v1v8_digital ~ v1v8_digital
power_interface.v1v8_analog ~ v1v8_analog
power_interface.v3v3_digital ~ v3v3_digital
power_interface.v5v_led ~ v5v_led
power_interface.gnd ~ gnd

powermodule.ato - the split analog/digital 1V8 domains and the dedicated LED rail, declared as code.

The honest difficulty is intent. A drawn schematic carries an enormous amount of visual and symbolic representation - orientation, grouping, the shapes of familiar sub-circuits - and that layer does real verification work: you can see that a rail is right. Code-driven hardware tools do not yet capture it, so manually checking the circuit, or recovering what the designer meant, is harder than it should be. Scalability and machine assistance argue for code; human verification still argues for the drawing.

Closing remarks

The interesting problems were density and power: two optical sites, a radio, a PMIC, flash, and a UI in a 23 mm outline forced the 6-layer stack and the 0201/BGA packaging, and keeping the PPG front end quiet drove the rail-domain separation.
