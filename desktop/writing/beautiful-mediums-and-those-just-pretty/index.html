Beautiful Mediums and Those Just Pretty; They Differ by Expressivity

A defense of communicating ideas through short-form video. People don't read anymore, and the canon has been replaced by internet jokes.

We used to be able to say a great deal in very little. You could gesture at Thoreau, or a psalm, or a line everyone half-remembered, and a whole world arrived with it — compression by shared reference. That's gone now. The common canon is dead — not by repression but by disuse; it's simply easier to reach for a reference from the internet.

The New Canon

So here is the defense, plainly. People barely read anymore — we're sliding from a literate society into a largely illiterate one, and the room the essay was read in is emptying out. I've decided to put my ideas where the people actually are: short-form video, the despised end of it included. I judge a medium by what it's capable of — how much it can express, how free it is inside and out, and how much it can compress through shared reference. By those criteria the disreputable little forms don't just hold up; they've quietly become the new canon — a living, common, self-renewing stock of reference, dense enough to say much in little. The essay was capable of all of it too. But its canon is gone and its readers are leaving, and the despised forms kept the one thing it lost.

Pretty, and Beautiful

Take the criteria one at a time, starting with the oldest: pretty versus beautiful. K-pop is capable of enormous polish — total production value, obvious effort — and almost no expressivity. It's impressive, and it's bland; you can admire every surface and feel nothing. That's pretty. Set it against the clavichord, a quiet parlor instrument on which you can nevertheless say a startling amount. Beauty isn't the finish. Beauty is how much a medium can actually carry. Polish is just polish.

Internal and External

Expressivity gets caged two ways: from inside the form, or from outside it. K-pop is caged internally — so manufactured that there's a ceiling on what it can say, whoever's at the controls. Mr. Beast is caged externally — not by the form but by the audience. Built for the largest possible crowd, it can't afford to offend anyone; it stays broadly agreeable, and broad agreeableness is a cage. Which is the uncomfortable part: a medium has to be able to disagree — to be disliked — before it can be beautiful. A form that can never offend can never say much.

The Art of Compression

And the criterion that actually brought me here: how much a medium can compress. A friend who makes this stuff for a living put it best — it's the art of compression, and compression is implicit by nature; it works by everything it doesn't have to say. That is what the canon used to do. "Thoreau" was a compression algorithm — one word that unpacked into a library everyone carried. We don't carry that library now, but the feed built its own and rebuilds it weekly: a churning stock of references and formats dense enough that a fifteen-second clip can carry an argument I'd need a paragraph to spell out. People already do it — there's a SpongeBob clip that delivers a whole thesis about a permanent underclass, in a form your grandmother would call garbage. That isn't the absence of depth. It's depth compressed past the point of respectability — and the disrepute is the cover that lets it say the thing at all.

A century or two ago you could publish an enormous book and people read it — and the same era made Sade write the 120 Days of Sodom on a scroll he hid in the wall of his cell. Things come and go.
